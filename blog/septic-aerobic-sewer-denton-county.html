The Lot — What Happens After the Drain

Septic, Aerobic, or Sewer: What Your Lot Needs in Denton County

If your lot can tie into a city sewer line, that's usually the simplest answer. If it can't, you'll need an on-site sewage facility — a septic system — designed from a soil test on your lot and permitted before construction. On the heavy clay common in this part of Texas, a standard gravel drainfield often isn't allowed, so many acreage homes end up with an aerobic system that sprays treated water on the lawn. That system comes with a maintenance contract and inspections for as long as you own it.

Nobody falls in love with a lot because of its wastewater plan. But on acreage in Denton and Tarrant County, "where does it go when you flush?" changes the site budget, the layout of the yard, what a seller's disclosure needs to tell you, and what you'll be doing every few months after you move in. It's a question to answer before you close on land, not after.

Here's how it actually works, in plain terms.

The Three Options in One Minute

  • City sewer. Your house drains into a public sewer main and the city treats it. You pay a tap fee and usually impact fees up front, then a monthly sewer bill. No tank, no spray heads, no maintenance contract.
  • Conventional septic. Wastewater settles in a tank, and the liquid flows out into a buried drainfield where the soil absorbs and filters it. Simple and low-maintenance, but it only works where the soil can actually absorb water.
  • Aerobic treatment with spray. A tank system pumps air into the wastewater so bacteria break it down much more completely, then disinfects it (usually with chlorine) and sprays it over a planted area of the yard through sprinkler heads. It works where a drainfield won't, but it has moving parts and needs regular service.

Texas calls any of the non-sewer options an on-site sewage facility, or OSSF. The rules are statewide, written by the Texas Commission on Environmental Quality (TCEQ) in Chapter 285 of the Texas Administrative Code, and enforced locally by counties and some cities acting as TCEQ's "authorized agents."

Step One: Is Sewer Available at the Lot?

Before anybody talks about tanks, find out whether a sewer main is reachable. Many lots inside city limits in the corridor have sewer at the street. Many acreage lots, especially in unincorporated areas, don't.

"Available" has a few layers:

  • Who serves the lot? Water and sewer in North Texas can come from a city, a utility district, or a private provider, and they're not always the same company. Ask the city or the seller for the provider, then call that provider directly.
  • How far is the main? A main across the road or down the block can mean boring under pavement or running a long service line, and that's on you.
  • Gravity or a pump? If the house sits lower than the main, you may need a small lift station to push wastewater uphill.
  • What are the fees? Tap fees and impact fees vary a lot from city to city. Get the current numbers in writing from the city before you budget.

If sewer is available, the city may require you to connect to it rather than install a septic system. Confirm the city's rule before planning anything else.

If There's No Sewer: Who Permits Your System

Who you deal with depends on where the lot sits, and it's not always obvious from the mailing address.

  • Unincorporated Denton County. Denton County Public Health's Environmental Health division permits septic systems outside city limits.
  • Inside a city in Denton County. Some cities handle septic permits themselves; the City of Denton, for example, has its own OSSF permit process. Check with the city's development or building department first.
  • Tarrant County. Tarrant County permits systems in the unincorporated areas and, by contract, in 21 cities, including Keller, Southlake, and Haslet. Fort Worth, Arlington, and Burleson run their own programs.

Whichever office it is, they're the ones who issue the permit, inspect the install, and keep the system's records on file for the life of the house.

How Big Does the Lot Have to Be?

State rules set a floor for new subdivisions using septic: lots of at least half an acre where public water serves the lot, and at least one acre where the home will be on a private water well. Older lots platted before those rules took effect can be treated differently.

Counties can go further. In Tarrant County, Commissioners Court adopted a rule in late 2025 that new single-family subdivisions on septic must have lots of at least one acre, net of drainage and floodplain easements. Fort Worth's extraterritorial jurisdiction, which covers much of Tarrant County, also requires a one-acre minimum.

Lot size isn't the only limit. The system has to fit on the lot with required distances from the house, property lines, any water well, and drainage features. A one-acre lot with a big house, a pool, a long driveway, and a creek easement can run out of room for a spray area faster than you'd think.

Why Clay Pushes So Many Lots to Aerobic

A conventional drainfield depends on the soil soaking up water. Heavy clay barely does. If you've read our post on post-tension slabs and North Texas clay, it's the same soil, causing a different problem: the clay that swells and shrinks under a foundation also won't take effluent quickly enough.

Texas sorts soil into classes for septic design, from sandy Class I up to clay-heavy Class IV. TCEQ's own guide rates standard absorptive drainfields, leaching chambers, and gravel-less pipe as unsuitable in Class IV soil. What's still allowed there includes:

  • Aerobic treatment with surface application (spray), which needs a vegetated area and disinfection
  • Drip irrigation, which spreads treated water through tubing just below the surface
  • Low-pressure dosing, evapotranspiration beds, and mounds

Of those, aerobic with spray is the one you'll see most often on acreage homes around here. But soils change across the county, and two lots on the same road can test differently. The soil test on your lot decides it, not the system next door.

How Permitting Works in Denton County

For a lot in unincorporated Denton County, the county's published process runs roughly like this:

  1. Other county permits first, if needed. A floodplain development permit and a culvert permit for the driveway may be required by the county's planning department before the septic permit.
  2. Site evaluation. A licensed professional engineer or a licensed site evaluator visits the lot, checks the soil, and maps where a system can go.
  3. Design. The system is designed from that evaluation to meet county and TCEQ rules and sized for the house.
  4. Permit application. Submitted in person with a site plan, the design, any required maintenance contract, and a fee. The county's current application lists $310 for a new system, and permits are often issued the same day.
  5. Installation and inspection. A licensed installer puts the system in, and county staff inspect it before the tanks are backfilled.
  6. Approval to use. The system can't be put into service until the owner has the county's notice of approval in hand.

Systems that require maintenance, which includes aerobic systems, also get a certification recorded in the county's deed records, so the next owner knows what they're buying. When the property sells, the permit transfers and the new owner has to get a signed maintenance contract to the county within 30 days.

Living With an Aerobic System

An aerobic system is a small treatment plant in your yard. It works well when it's maintained, and Texas rules make sure it is.

  • Two years of service come with it. A new aerobic system must have an initial two-year maintenance contract with a licensed maintenance provider, usually arranged by the installer, starting when the system is first used.
  • Inspections at least every four months. The provider checks the system and files a report with the permitting office at least three times a year. Systems with approved electronic monitoring can drop to twice a year.
  • After two years, Denton County still wants a contract. State rules let homeowners of single-family homes maintain their own system after the first two years. Denton County is stricter: owners must keep a signed contract with a maintenance company continuously, or have written approval for homeowner training.
  • Chlorine is your job. Most aerobic systems disinfect with chlorine tablets in a feeder. TCEQ says to use calcium hypochlorite tablets certified for wastewater, not swimming pool tablets. Store them away from the house and away from metal tools.
  • The spray area is part of the system. Keep it grassed or planted, keep kids and pets off it when it's spraying, and don't grow vegetables there; Texas rules prohibit applying effluent to vegetable gardens.

Day to day, the habits that keep it healthy are simple: spread laundry out over the week instead of doing it all on Saturday, go easy on the garbage disposal, keep wipes and excess paper out of the toilets, and limit antibacterial products that kill the bacteria the system depends on. If the sprinklers come on while it's raining, rainwater may be getting into the tank, and that's worth a call to your provider.

“The wastewater plan should be settled before the house is designed, not squeezed in after the pool and the driveway take the good ground.”

What It Means for Your Budget

We're not going to quote you a septic price in a blog post, because the honest answer depends on the soil report, the size of the house, the type of system, and how far it sits from the house. What we can tell you is where the money goes:

  • Up front with sewer: tap fee, impact fees, and the service line from the house to the main, which can be significant if the main is far away or across a road.
  • Up front with septic: site evaluation, design, permit, and installation. Aerobic and other advanced systems cost more than a conventional tank and drainfield.
  • Ongoing with sewer: a monthly sewer bill.
  • Ongoing with aerobic: the maintenance contract, chlorine, electricity for the aerator and pump, periodic pumping of solids, and eventually replacement parts like the aerator or spray heads.

The mistake we see is treating any of this as a line item to figure out after closing. Our custom home cost guide breaks out site work as its own category for exactly this reason. It's one of the places where two lots at the same price can end up tens of thousands of dollars apart.

Questions to Answer Before You Buy the Lot

  1. Is there a public sewer main that can serve this lot, who owns it, and what are the tap and impact fees?
  2. If not, which office permits septic here: the county or the city?
  3. Is the lot large enough under state and county rules, given whether it will have public water or a well?
  4. Has a site evaluation or soil test been done? If not, can you get one during your option period?
  5. Where would a spray area or drainfield go, and does that leave room for the house, pool, driveway, and outbuildings you want?
  6. Are there floodplain, drainage, or easement areas that limit where things can go?
  7. If it's an existing system, is it permitted, and is there a current maintenance contract and inspection history?

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a septic system to build in Denton County?
Only if your lot can't connect to a public sewer. Many lots inside city limits have sewer service; many acreage lots in unincorporated areas don't. If there's no sewer, you'll need a permitted on-site sewage facility designed from a soil evaluation on your lot.
Why do so many North Texas homes have aerobic septic systems?
Much of the area has clay soil that absorbs water too slowly for a standard drainfield. TCEQ rates standard absorptive drainfields as unsuitable in clay-heavy Class IV soil, so aerobic systems that treat wastewater and spray it on a planted area are a common alternative.
Who issues septic permits in Denton County?
In unincorporated Denton County, Denton County Public Health's Environmental Health division issues on-site sewage facility permits. Inside city limits, check with the city, since some cities run their own septic permitting.
How often does an aerobic septic system have to be inspected in Texas?
A licensed maintenance provider inspects it at least once every four months and reports to the permitting office. New systems come with a two-year initial maintenance contract, and Denton County requires owners to keep a maintenance contract in place after that unless they have approval for homeowner training.
What is the minimum lot size for a septic system in Texas?
For new subdivisions, state rules require at least half an acre when the lot has public water and at least one acre when it uses a private well. Some counties require more; Tarrant County requires one acre, net of drainage and floodplain easements, for new subdivisions on septic.
