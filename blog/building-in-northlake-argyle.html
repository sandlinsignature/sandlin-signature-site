The Corridor Guide — Northlake · Argyle · Denton County

Building a Custom Home in Northlake & Argyle ISD

The North DFW corridor is one of the last places in the metroplex where you can still buy an acre homesite in a top-rated school district and build exactly what you want on it. Land in the Northlake and Argyle area generally runs $150,000 to $400,000+ depending on size, utilities, and school zoning — and the lot you choose will shape your build budget more than any finish decision you make later.

Why This Corridor

Drive twenty minutes in most directions from Dallas or Fort Worth and the lots get smaller, the setbacks get tighter, and the homes get closer together. That's not a criticism of production neighborhoods — it's just what land economics do as a metro fills in.

The Northlake and Argyle corridor is different for a simple reason: it filled in later. There is still real acreage here, still land that hasn't been optioned by a national builder, and still a path to a home built around how your family actually lives rather than a floor plan picked off a wall.

That window is narrowing. Production builders are actively acquiring larger tracts across Denton County, and every year the remaining acre homesites get harder to find and more expensive to buy.

"The lot decision is the build decision. People spend six months choosing finishes and six days choosing land — and the land is what they can't change later."

Argyle ISD and the School Zoning Trap

Argyle ISD is one of the highest-rated districts in North Texas, and for most families building here it's the single biggest draw. But there is a detail that catches buyers regularly:

City limits and school zoning are not the same thing. Parts of Northlake are zoned to Argyle ISD. Parts are zoned to Northwest ISD. Both are good districts, but they are not interchangeable, and the difference can move a property's value meaningfully.

Zoning in this corridor is determined lot by lot. A mailing address that says "Northlake" tells you very little about which schools the children living there will attend. Confirm the specific parcel with the district before you write an offer on land — not after.

What Land Actually Costs Here

Land pricing in the corridor varies more than buyers expect, driven by four things: lot size, school zoning, whether utilities are at the street, and whether the lot sits inside a developed community or on raw acreage.

Lot TypeTypical RangeWhat Drives It
Community homesite, under 1 acre $150,000–$250,000 Utilities at the street, HOA infrastructure in place, shortest path to permit
Acre homesite in an established community $250,000–$400,000 Argyle ISD zoning and acreage carry a premium; site work is usually predictable
Raw acreage, 2+ acres Varies widely Price per acre drops, but septic, well, driveway, and utility runs can add six figures

These are planning ranges as of 2026, not quotes. The useful takeaway isn't the number — it's that a cheaper lot is frequently the more expensive project once site work is priced.

Site Work: The Line Item Nobody Budgets For

On a finished community lot, site work is relatively contained. On rural acreage it is the most underestimated cost in the entire build, and it is almost always discovered after someone has already bought the land.

What drives it:

  • Utilities. Is water at the street or does the lot need a well? Is there sewer, or does it need a septic or aerobic treatment system? Running electric several hundred feet is not a small number.
  • Soil and slope. North Texas clay moves. Foundation engineering is not the place to save money, and a sloped lot can add significant cost before a slab is ever poured.
  • Access. A long driveway is a real construction cost, and it also determines whether concrete trucks and delivery vehicles can reach the build site.
  • Clearing. Mature trees are an asset. Removing them, or building around them, is a budget line either way.
Get a builder on the lot before you close on it. An hour walking the property can surface tens of thousands of dollars in site work that no listing sheet will ever mention. Most builders will do this at no cost, and it costs you nothing to ask.

Permitting and Timeline

Municipal review timelines in Denton County are reasonable compared to some parts of the metroplex, but they are not instant, and they vary by jurisdiction. Plan on design and permitting occupying real months on the front end before ground is ever broken.

For a typical custom home in this corridor, budget roughly 12 to 16 months from signed plans to move-in, with design and permitting ahead of that. Builders who quote dramatically shorter timelines are usually describing a best case that assumes nothing goes wrong — and on a custom build, something always does.

The variables that move a schedule most: utility connections, weather during foundation and framing, long-lead finish selections, and how quickly decisions get made. That last one is more in your control than most people realize.

Build vs. Buy in This Corridor

Honest answer: it depends on what you're comparing.

If you already own land here, or you've found a lot you love, building is a strong case. You control the plan, the finishes, and the quality of what's behind the walls — and you're building into a corridor where acreage is getting scarcer, not more plentiful.

If you're comparing against resale, run the numbers carefully. You're not just buying a house; you're buying exactly what you want, on a timeline that's measured in months rather than weeks. Some families are ready for that, and some aren't. A builder who tells you building is always the right answer isn't being straight with you.

There's also a middle path people forget: a finished spec home gets you custom-level construction without the timeline or the decision fatigue. If the plan works for your family, it's the fastest route to a home that was actually built well.

Questions to Ask Before You Buy Land

  • What school district is this specific parcel zoned to, confirmed with the district?
  • Is water at the street, or does the lot need a well?
  • Is there sewer, or will it need septic or aerobic treatment?
  • How far is the electric run, and who pays for it?
  • Has anyone done a soil test, and what does the foundation engineering look like?
  • Are there deed restrictions, HOA architectural requirements, or a required builder list?
  • Is there a floodplain, easement, or pipeline crossing any part of the property?

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Northlake in Argyle ISD?
Parts of Northlake are zoned to Argyle ISD and parts are zoned to Northwest ISD. School zoning in this corridor is determined lot by lot, not by city limits, so always confirm the specific address with the district before you buy land.
Can you still find acre lots in the Northlake and Argyle area?
Yes. Acre and near-acre homesites are still available across the corridor, which is increasingly rare closer to Dallas and Fort Worth. Availability is tightening as production builders acquire larger tracts, so the window is narrowing.
How long does it take to build a custom home in Northlake or Argyle?
Plan on roughly 12 to 16 months from signed plans to move-in for a typical custom build in this corridor, plus design and permitting time on the front end. Site conditions, utility access, and municipal review timelines are the biggest variables.
What site work costs should I expect on a rural North DFW lot?
Site work is the single most underestimated line item on rural lots. Depending on slope, soil, tree clearing, driveway length, and whether the lot needs a septic system, aerobic treatment, or a water well, site work commonly runs from the low tens of thousands into six figures.
Do I need a septic system or well in Northlake?
It depends entirely on the lot. Some homesites in the corridor have municipal water and sewer at the street; others require a septic or aerobic system and, less commonly, a water well. Confirm this before you close on land, because it materially changes the budget.
