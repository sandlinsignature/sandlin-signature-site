The Land Owner’s Guide — Denton County

Building on Your Land in Denton County

If you own land in Denton County, the cost of building on it comes down to a short list of things you can check before you design a single room: who issues your permits, how wastewater gets handled, whether any of it is in a floodplain, and what it takes to get water, power, and a driveway to the house.

The Short Version

Buying land is the easy part. What decides whether a custom home on that land costs what you expect is a short list of things most people never check until a builder walks the lot: who has jurisdiction, how wastewater gets handled, whether any of it is in a floodplain, what it takes to get water and power to the house pad, and what the deed restrictions allow.

None of these are exotic. All of them are knowable before you design a single room. This is the checklist we run on every piece of land a client brings us in Denton County.

1. City, ETJ, or County? Find Out Who Has Jurisdiction

The town name on your mailing address doesn't tell you who issues your permits. A tract with an Argyle, Justin, Krum, or Sanger address might sit inside a city, inside a city's extraterritorial jurisdiction, or in plain unincorporated county.

  • Inside city limits: the city's building codes, inspections, setbacks, and often minimum home sizes or masonry requirements apply. Utilities are more likely to be at the street.
  • Unincorporated county: Denton County requires a development permit for each structure, submitted with a site plan that shows the house and its distance to at least two property lines. There is far less design regulation, but you take on more of the infrastructure yourself.

The appraisal district record for your parcel lists the taxing units, which is a good first clue. Confirm with the city or county before you budget.

2. Septic: The Line Item That Moves Budgets

If sewer isn't available at the property, the home needs an on-site sewage facility. In unincorporated Denton County, the process runs through the county:

  • A site evaluation and soil classification done by a licensed professional engineer or licensed site evaluator you hire.
  • A system design that shows the system fits on the property and meets county and TCEQ rules. All wastewater has to be treated and disposed of on your own land.
  • A permit, then an inspection before anything is buried, then approval before the system goes into use.

North Texas clay often pushes lots toward aerobic systems rather than a conventional tank and drainfield. Aerobic systems work well, but they cost more and the county requires an ongoing signed maintenance contract. The soil, the size of the house, and where the system can physically fit all affect the number, so this is something to price before you fall in love with a floor plan.

3. Floodplain: Check the Map, Then Check the Pad

Plenty of beautiful Denton County acreage has a creek running through it, and that creek usually comes with a FEMA flood zone. Being partly in a floodplain doesn't make land unbuildable. It changes where the house can go and what paperwork follows it.

  • If the home sits in a FEMA Special Flood Hazard Area, the county requires a finished floor elevation certificate from a registered surveyor, engineer, or architect after construction.
  • Filling, digging, or building ponds in a flood zone requires its own permit.
  • Floodplain status affects insurance cost and, eventually, resale.

The best answer is usually simple: find the highest, driest buildable pad and keep the house, septic field, and driveway out of the mapped zone.

4. Getting Water, Power, and a Driveway to the House

Raw land prices rarely include the cost of reaching the home site. Before you buy, or before you design, find out:

  • Water: Is there a rural water supply line at the road, and what's the tap fee and capacity? If not, you're looking at a well.
  • Electric: How far is the nearest service? A long run from the road to a house set deep on the property adds real cost.
  • Driveway: Length, base, and whether a culvert is needed where it meets the road. On larger tracts, a few hundred extra feet of driveway is one of the most underestimated costs.
  • Gas and internet: Often not available on rural tracts. Plan for propane and check service providers early.

5. Deed Restrictions and HOA Rules

Many rural subdivisions across the county carry deed restrictions even without an active HOA. Common ones include minimum square footage, masonry percentage, roof pitch, outbuilding rules, and limits on manufactured or barn-style homes. Ask the title company for the restrictions before you close, and read them before you design.

6. If the Land Has an Ag Exemption

A lot of the acreage changing hands in Denton County carries agricultural or wildlife valuation, which keeps the taxes low. Building a home takes the house site out of agricultural use, which can trigger rollback taxes on that portion. We cover how that works in our guide to building a home on land with an ag exemption.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a permit to build a house in unincorporated Denton County?

Yes. Denton County requires a development permit for each structure, with a site plan showing the improvements and their distances to the property lines. If septic is needed, that's a separate permit after the development permit.

Who designs a septic system in Denton County?

The site evaluation and soil classification must be done by a licensed professional engineer or a licensed site evaluator that you hire. The design has to show the system fits on the property and meets county and state rules.

Can I build on land that's partly in a floodplain?

Usually, yes, as long as the house is placed correctly. If any part of the home sits in a FEMA Special Flood Hazard Area, the county requires an elevation certificate after construction, and filling or digging in the flood zone needs its own permit.

When should I talk to a builder about my land?

Before you design, and ideally before you close. A builder can tell you where the house should sit, what the site work will cost, and whether your budget and the land line up.
